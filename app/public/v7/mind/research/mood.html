Mood check-in

What happens when a patient taps their mood check-in on the Mind screen: how the sheet asks, whether a tap is enough to save, what we say back on a low day, and how the row looks afterwards. Five variants below, each running on the real Mind screen with the coach's "Calmer days" plan (two check-ins, 1:00 PM and 8:30 PM). A state switcher above each screen jumps to the happy path, the low path and the edge cases; everything inside is live.

The question, in Shaheer's words"When Moodcheckin task is clicked then in bottom sheet how do we ask Mood then does it require confirmation or How do we encourage/motivate etc when they are sad or what kind of communication shall be there."
Already decided: the sheet asks the mood question only, with no follow-up questions. After the answer comes a short, warm reply that says what to do or offers support. When the mood is low, it gently suggests one practice from the patient's own plan (a breathing exercise or a me-time activity), right there in the sheet. Checking in earns the points; the answer is never scored.

The answers in short

1. How to ask

One question, "How are you feeling?", answered with five faces, each with its word underneath (variant A). One tap. Faces carry meaning across languages, and the word under each removes any doubt about what a face means.

2. Confirmation

Picking a face is a draft. KAIRA's card shimmers in and answers it, and only "Log Mood" logs the mood and adds its points. The sheet closes and a toast says "Mood logged. Thank you for checking in." Reopened, the sheet shows the logged face and reply with Done; picking another face brings back Log Mood. Each check-in slot keeps its own answer.

3. What to say back

A short KAIRA message on an indigo card, one fixed template per face. Low and very low: comfort first, then one general thing that helps, in words only ("When you're ready, five minutes of slow breathing settles your body first, and the mind often follows."). No practice card.

4. Encouragement

The same Log Mood step and the same toast for every face, and the hint "Every check-in counts the same." Replies for good days point at noticing what helped, so a happy answer earns nothing extra.

5. "What shaped today" tags

Dropped. The sheet asks one question only. Causes, if the coach needs them, belong in the coach's call or a weekly reflection.

6. End state

The row keeps its title, "Mood check-in", and its time, and gets a tick. The line under it becomes the answer: "🙂 Good, at 1:12 PM". With two check-ins each row shows its own answer; a passed slot reads "You can still check in today" in grey.

The problem

Who. A person in India living with type 2 diabetes or obesity, usually 35 to 60, on a GoodFlip program. Many read English slowly or prefer Hindi or another language. Many use a mid-range Android phone, often with one hand, sometimes with reading glasses away.

When. Once or twice a day at a time their coach set, most often after lunch and after dinner. They arrive from a reminder or from the Mind screen's plan, between other things: the kitchen, the office, the TV.

State of mind. Anything from fine to drained. Diabetes distress is common in India, and so is a low mood (figures under Evidence). A low answer will be frequent, so the low path is a main path and has to feel calm and useful. A patient who taps a sad face and gets a cheerful line, or a phone number, learns to tap "Okay" next time.

What the check-in is for. A few seconds of noticing how they feel, a kind word back, and a pattern over weeks that the patient and coach can see. The score rewards showing up, so the answer can be honest.

Scenarios

Every case the sheet and the row have to handle, grouped. The variants below are built against this list.

Opening the check-in

  1. Patient taps the Mood check-in row on the Mind screen.
  2. Patient taps the reminder notification at the slot time and lands in the open sheet.
  3. Plan has one check-in (evening) or two (often afternoon and evening).
  4. Patient opens the later slot before its time.
  5. Patient opens a slot whose time has passed, later the same day.
  6. Patient opens yesterday's check-in from history: read only.

Answering

  1. Taps a face: saved at once, the sheet stays and answers.
  2. Taps a different face: the answer updates in place.
  3. Closes the sheet without answering: nothing saved, the row is unchanged.
  4. Closes after answering: the answer is kept, the row shows it.
  5. No internet when tapping: the choice stays marked, an inline message offers Try Again.
  6. Reopens later the same day and changes the answer: it still counts once.

What the app says back

  1. Very low, low, okay, good, great: one fixed reply each.
  2. Very low: comfort, then slow breathing in words.
  3. Low: comfort, then something small they enjoy, in words.
  4. The reply shimmers in first, as KAIRA reads the answer, and changes when another face is picked.
  5. The toast after Log Mood reads the same for every answer.
  6. Several very low days in a row (see Open questions).

Logging

  1. Log Mood stays disabled until a face is picked, and while KAIRA's card shimmers.
  2. Closing the sheet before Log Mood logs nothing.
  3. Offline: Log Mood fails, the error shows above it, and the button reads Try Again.

On the Mind screen afterwards

  1. One check-in done: the row ticks and shows the face, the word and the time.
  2. Two check-ins, first done: the second waits at its time.
  3. Both done: each row shows its own answer.
  4. A slot passed unanswered: "You can still check in today", in neutral grey.
  5. The Mind score rises the same for any face.

Across days

  1. At midnight both slots reset to empty.
  2. Past answers are kept for the weekly pattern.
  3. The coach sees the answers (see Open questions).

Evidence

Naming a feeling calms itPutting a feeling into a word lowered the amygdala's response to upsetting images in brain scans (Lieberman and colleagues, 2007). The word under each face does this job, in the same tap.
Low answers will be commonIn Indian adults with type 2 diabetes, 53% had diabetes distress and 20% depression in one 1,925-person study; a review of 59 Indian studies put depression at 38%, higher in women (39%) than men (31%).
One question worksSingle items were used in 92% of studies measuring daily low mood in older adults, and adherence ran from 61% to 95% with brief, predefined answers. A one-tap question fits this audience.
Five minutes of breathing lifts moodIn a month-long trial, five minutes a day of slow, exhale-led breathing (cyclic sighing) improved mood more than mindfulness meditation (Balban and colleagues, 2023).
Doing something you enjoy lifts moodBehavioural activation, scheduling pleasant activities, is an effective way to lift low mood across many trials (Cuijpers and colleagues, 2007). It is what the plan's me-time already is.

Variants

Every variant asks the one question and then replies. They differ in two ways only: how the question is answered (five faces, a slider, a row of words, three faces) and how the reply reads (a KAIRA message or a quiet line). Each screen below is the real Mind screen with the variant switched on. Tap anywhere: the Mood check-in rows, the faces, then Log Mood (it logs the mood and moves the Mind score). The link under each screen opens it full size.

Recommendation

Variant A, five faces and a KAIRA message. One tap answers and saves, so the check-in takes two seconds, and the saved line is identical for every face. The word under each face makes the answer clear to someone reading slowly, and it is the patient naming their own feeling. KAIRA's reply is short, warm and specific: on a low day it comforts first, then gives one reason a practice helps, then puts that practice one tap away.

The catch: KAIRA speaking on every check-in, twice a day, can start to feel like a lot on good days. If it does, mix A and B: KAIRA's message on low and very low, B's quiet line on okay, good and great (open question 6). Face art should come from Figma; the emoji here are placeholders.

What we say back

Fixed templates, one per answer. KAIRA's message (A, C, D, E) opens with how she takes the answer, and on a low day adds one plain reason the practice helps. B's quiet line is the app speaking, one sentence. No contractions, so the lines translate cleanly.

Lines every answer shares

  • Hint, before answering: "Pick the one closest to how you feel. Every check-in counts the same."
  • Button: "Log Mood". After logging, a toast: "Mood logged. Thank you for checking in." Identical for every answer.
  • Offline: "Could not log your mood. Check your internet and try again." with Try Again.
  • Row after: "🙂 Good, at 1:12 PM". A passed slot: "You can still check in today".

Open questions

1. Should several very low days in a row lead to something more?

  1. Nothing more. The sheet stays the same every day. Simplest, but a patient who is struggling goes unseen until the next coach call.
  2. The coach sees a quiet flag. After three very low or low answers in seven days, the coach portal marks the patient; the patient's screen is unchanged and the coach reaches out in their usual way.Recommended Keeps the sheet gentle and puts a person in the loop.
  3. KAIRA's message changes. On the third low day in a week, her reply adds: "You have had a few heavy days. Your coach Sahana would be glad to hear from you." with a Message Sahana link under the practice. Visible to the patient, which some may find intrusive.
  4. A quiet helpline line on very low only. Under the practice, in small text: "Need someone to talk to right now? Tele-MANAS, 14416, free, any time." There for the rare emergency, never the main path.

2. With two check-ins, how does the mood score count?

  1. Half for each. Both slots matter equally.Recommended
  2. Either one earns it all. How the screen scores today. Kinder on busy days, but the second check-in then earns nothing and will fade.
  3. All for the first, the second is a bonus without points. Clear, but the evening slot becomes optional by design.

3. Can a check-in be done before its time?

  1. Any time today. How these screens work now. Both slots could be answered at 1 PM, which defeats the second.
  2. From the start of its part of the day. The afternoon slot opens at 12 PM, the evening slot at 5 PM; earlier, the row reads "Opens at 5 PM".Recommended
  3. Only within two hours of its time. Most accurate, and the most likely to be missed.

4. Which language for the labels and replies?

  1. English only. Faces carry the meaning.
  2. Follow the app's language setting. Labels and KAIRA's replies are all translated.Recommended
  3. English with Hindi under it. Helps mixed readers, crowds the face row.

5. Who sees the answer?

  1. The coach sees each check-in, and the sheet says so once, in the hint line: "Your coach sees your check-ins."Recommended Honest, and it makes the flag in question 1 possible.
  2. The coach sees the weekly pattern only.
  3. Private to the patient. More candid answers, and no way for anyone to notice a hard week.

6. Who speaks the reply?

  1. KAIRA, every time (A). Warm and consistent; can feel like a lot on good days.
  2. A quiet line, every time (B). Light; on a hard day it can feel thin.
  3. KAIRA on low and very low, the quiet line on okay and better. Support where it matters, lightness where it does not.Recommended

7. Face art

  1. Emoji as in today's prototype. They look different on every phone.
  2. GoodFlip's own face set from Figma, in one style, calm colours, never red for sad.Recommended
